Lab 20 · Pricing by simulation

GPU Monte Carlo

A worst-of barrier reverse convertible has no pricing formula. The desk simulates the market instead: millions of possible years, each one a day-by-day path for every underlying. Here your graphics card runs those paths in parallel and the price settles in front of you.

Scope:One-year note, strike 100%, knock-in put on the worst of one to three underlyings with equal volatility and equal pairwise correlation. Lognormal paths with 252 daily steps, no dividends, no skew, no issuer funding spread or margin. Continuous monitoring uses a Brownian-bridge correction per underlying, which is exact for one name and a close approximation for a basket. Lock-in is tested on every daily close of the worst performer and, once hit, repays par at maturity. Autocall is tested on the quarterly closes and repays par; the coupon accrues and is paid on redemption in both cases. Illustrative only.

    Why the GPU

    Race the CPU

    Every path is independent, so thousands of GPU threads can each take one. The CPU version runs the identical algorithm in a background thread. Both use the same random numbers, so on the same paths they must give the same answer.

    For developers

    What runs on the GPU

    One compute shader, written in WGSL. Each thread owns one path: it seeds its own random-number generator from its path number, steps every underlying through 252 days, then the 256 threads in a workgroup add their results together on chip. The browser reads back one small sum per 256 paths.

    1. SeedHash the path number into a PCG generator
    2. ShockBox-Muller normals, correlated by a Cholesky factor
    3. StepAdvance log prices; test the barrier; bridge between closes
    4. PayWorst-of put payoff, weighted by knock-in odds
    5. ReduceTree-sum 256 paths in workgroup memory
    Show the WGSL kernel

    Why simulate at all?

    With one stock, a continuously monitored knock-in put has a closed-form price, and the simulation lands on it. Add a second or third stock and the worst-of has no formula, so desks price it by averaging over simulated paths.

    What the client has sold

    The coupon is the interest on the money plus the premium for the knock-in put the client has written. More names, lower correlation, higher volatility or a higher barrier all make that put dearer, and the coupon rises with it.

    Lock-in and autocall

    A lock-in gives protection back to the client after a rally, so it costs coupon. An autocall takes the note away in the same good states. The put the client has sold shrinks a little, but the coupon is now earned over a shorter life, so the annual rate usually rises. That matches the direction on the desk’s own feature price list, though without funding spread or margin the size here is larger.

    Observation matters

    Checking the barrier only at the daily close misses intraday dips, so the put is cheaper and the coupon a little lower. The one-stock closed form adjusts for this by shifting the barrier down slightly.

    Method references

    The one-stock reference is the Reiner-Rubinstein formula for a continuously monitored down-and-in put. For daily-close monitoring the barrier is shifted by the Broadie-Glasserman-Kou continuity correction, B·exp(−0.5826·σ·√Δt). Between closes, the chance that a lognormal path touched the barrier is the Brownian-bridge probability exp(−2·ln(S₀/B)·ln(S₁/B) / (σ²Δt)). Random numbers come from the 32-bit PCG hash described by Jarzynski and Olano (2020), and the kernel follows the W3C WebGPU and WGSL specifications.
