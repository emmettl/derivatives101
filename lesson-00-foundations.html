The one idea

Structured products are recipes, not ingredients

Unwrap a structured product and you usually find a bond or deposit plus one or more options. In a yield-enhancement product, the investor is commonly the net seller of an option. The premium received funds the headline coupon or discount; issuer margin and hedging costs come out before the client sees the result.

If you can name the option the client sold, you can explain most of the product’s risk.
Bond floor

The present value set aside to fund a promised repayment at maturity.

Option premium

The price of transferring an uncertain future payoff between buyer and seller.

Building blocks

A right for one side is an obligation for the other

A call gives its buyer the right to buy at the strike. A put gives its buyer the right to sell. The seller receives premium, but must perform if the buyer exercises. That asymmetry is the economic engine underneath the course.

Volatility measures movement, not direction. More expected movement makes both calls and puts more valuable. A product offering a richer coupon in a frightened market is not necessarily better value: the investor is being paid more because the obligation sold is worth more.

Payoff versus path

Where it ends is not always enough

A payoff diagram maps the final underlying level to redemption. A path chart shows whether something happened before maturity. A continuously monitored knock-in can switch on downside after a single touch, even if the underlying later recovers. A knock-out or autocall can end the trade before its stated maturity.

Coupon barrier

Decides whether one scheduled coupon is paid.

Protection barrier

Decides whether principal can follow the underlying down.

Autocall level

Decides whether the whole note redeems early.

Worst-of basket

Uses the weakest constituent, making the strongest names irrelevant.
